*Adapted from a bonus paper I wrote for a primatology course in spring 2019.*

For as long as we have been asking what makes us human, the great apes have hogged the spotlight. Chimpanzees use tools. Gorillas learn sign language. Orangutans have better hair than most of us and a fraction of the anxiety. We look at them, they look back at us, and everyone agrees: family.

Meanwhile the macaque, a monkey that has conquered more of the planet than any primate except us, has been sitting in a lab being extremely useful and receiving zero credit.

I took a primatology course in 2019 taught by Biruté Galdikas, who has spent the better part of her life studying orangutans. For the bonus paper, I chose to argue to her, in writing, that humans might be closer to monkeys than to apes. I stand by it. The copy in my Drive has fourteen margin comments, nine of which just say "passive," and I stand by that less.

## Why do apes get all the attention?

Part of it is genetics, and genetics is not wrong. Apes really are our closest relatives on paper, and our lineage split from the monkeys' roughly 25 million years ago. But part of it is just convenience.

There's a well-known bias in research called the streetlight effect: you look for your keys under the streetlight, not because that's where you dropped them, but because that's where you can see. Great apes are big, relatively slow, and spend a lot of time on the ground, so you can follow them around the forest and watch what they do. Monkeys live in the trees, move fast, and need complex three-dimensional habitats you cannot recreate in any enclosure. So we have mountains of ape literature and comparatively little on the social nuance of monkeys in the wild. Not because monkeys have less going on, but because they're harder to watch.

There's also an aesthetic bias. Apes stand up and look sort of like us. Monkeys walk on all fours and have tails. It is hard to feel kinship with something that has a tail.

## Closest to us... according to whom?

If you put a geneticist, a psychologist, and an ecologist in a room and asked "which primate is closest to humans?", you would get three answers and possibly a fight. The geneticist says chimpanzee. The ecologist might say something that thrives in as many environments as we do. As a student of neuropsychology, my definition of human-like leans on social behaviour, cognition, and the brain. So that's the lens I'm using, and here's what it turns up.

## Macaques colonize, like we do

Macaques are found across a wider geographic range than any other primate except humans (Thierry, 2007). Tropical forests, temperate forests, subalpine mountains, tidal areas, arid land, farmland, and cities. There are macaques living in snow in Japan, famously bathing in the Jigokudani hot springs to stay warm through the winter (Hori et al., 1977), which is the most relatable thing any nonhuman primate has ever done. That same species has also been one of the best-studied models of same-sex sexual behaviour in primates, in ways that extend to humans (Vasey, 1995; Dixson, 2010).

Most great apes, by contrast, occupy a fairly narrow set of habitats and have not expanded much beyond them. Evolutionary biologists have increasingly argued that robustness, a species' ability to stay stable and keep its systems working across wildly different environments, is its own marker of success (Thierry, 2007). By that measure, the macaque is the second most successful primate on Earth. Guess who's first.

## Macaques do politics, like we do

This is where it gets fun. Macaque societies are organized around dominance hierarchies and strong kin favouritism, which is to say nepotism. But conflict among macaques is rarely one-sided. Aggression is usually met with protest or retaliation, and sometimes followed by reconciliation (Thierry, 2007). Under the main hierarchy sit countless smaller alliances, each shaped by every individual's personal history with the troop.

My favourite detail: a macaque mother manages her offspring's social network by controlling who is allowed to approach them. She is curating her kid's connections before the kid can walk. Primatologists call this kind of social maneuvering *Machiavellian intelligence*, and Dario Maestripieri wrote a whole book about it in rhesus macaques titled *Macachiavellian Intelligence* (Maestripieri, 2007), which is the best pun in primatology and I will not be taking questions.

Troops typically range from 20 to 40 individuals and have been reported as large as 100 to 200. Keeping track of that many relationships, grudges, and alliances requires persistent social memory and the ability to recognize a lot of individuals. That is very human, and it is not something great apes, living in much smaller groups, are really put to the test on.

## Okay, but their brains

Here is where I have to be honest with myself. Rhesus macaques have been the workhorse of neuroscience for decades, and some of the regions behind our fanciest social cognition, like the medial prefrontal cortex and frontal pole, show real organizational parallels between macaques and humans (Sallet et al., 2013).

But the differences are big. The prefrontal cortex makes up about 28.5% of the neocortex in humans and about 11.3% in macaques, and neurons in layer III of the human prefrontal cortex carry roughly 70% more dendritic spines (Passingham, 2009). Macaques are an extremely good model of the human brain. They are not a miniature one. The catch is that we have done so little comparable work on great ape brains that nobody can say for sure the apes would score much closer.

## Also, they went to space first

A rhesus macaque named Albert was the first primate launched on a rocket, in 1948. He did not survive, and neither did several of his successors. In 1959, another rhesus macaque named Able made it to space and back alive, then died days later from a reaction to anesthesia during surgery to remove an electrode, which is the single most macaque sequence of events I can imagine. They have been adapting to thoroughly depressing, nonarboreal conditions on our behalf ever since.

## So, are we closer to monkeys?

It depends on who you ask, and that's the point. We lean so hard on genetic similarity that we rarely weigh anything else. But what actually made humans so distinctive wasn't intelligence alone. It was near-total domination of the planet, and that came from robustness, colonization, and the political skill to organize very large groups of individuals who don't all like each other.

The great apes are brilliant, and most of them are endangered. Intelligence alone wasn't enough to keep them safe. The macaque, meanwhile, is one of the very few animals besides us that operates like an army: in numbers, across continents, running on alliances and grudges.

If the question is who's most like us, maybe we should stop asking who looks most like us in a photo and start asking who's most likely to show up in our cities, take over our hot springs, and hold a grudge about it.
